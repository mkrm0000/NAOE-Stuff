Module 1: Heat Engine Fundamentals & Classification
Definition A heat engine is a device that converts thermal energy (from fuel combustion) into mechanical work. They are broadly classified into Internal Combustion (IC) engines and External Combustion (EC) engines based on where the combustion takes place.
Theory [Short]
  • IC Engine: Combustion occurs inside the engine cylinder/combustion chamber. The working fluid is the high‑pressure combustion gases themselves.
  • EC Engine: Combustion occurs outside the engine in a furnace/boiler. A separate working fluid (steam, air) is heated externally and then used to produce work.
Theory [Explained Simply] — IC vs. EC (The "Why")

Internal Combustion (IC) Engine: Like a firecracker exploding inside a heavy metal cylinder. The explosion happens right where the work is produced, directly pushing the piston.

External Combustion (EC) Engine: Like a fireplace boiling a big water kettle outside. The fire burns in a separate furnace, turning water to high‑pressure steam, which is piped over to push a piston or spin a turbine.

Memory Trick: IC vs. EC — Mnemonic: IN vs. OUT. Visual Story: IC = Inside Cylinder (a compact diesel engine). EC = External Campfire (a massive steam locomotive with a coal firebox under a water boiler).

Module 2: 2‑Stroke vs. 4‑Stroke Cycles & Marine Engine Classification
Definition 4‑Stroke Cycle: The piston completes four strokes (intake, compression, power, exhaust) and the crankshaft makes two full revolutions for every one power stroke. 2‑Stroke Cycle: The piston completes two strokes (one up, one down) and the crankshaft makes one full revolution for every one power stroke — intake and exhaust are combined.
Theory [Short]
  • 4‑Stroke Cycle: Phrase — "Suck, Squeeze, Bang, Blow" (Intake, Compression, Power, Exhaust). Takes 4 piston movements to get 1 power push.
  • 2‑Stroke Cycle: Rhyme — "One revolution around, a power bang is found!" (n=N power strokes per minute). Every single landing and push gives a power bounce.
Theory [Explained Simply]

4‑Stroke Cycle: Like riding a bicycle where you pedal, coast, pedal, coast. It takes 4 up‑and‑down piston movements (2 full pedal revolutions) to get 1 power push.

2‑Stroke Cycle: Like bouncing on a pogo stick where every single landing and push gives you a power bounce (1 full revolution per power push).

Module 3: Air‑Standard Thermodynamic Cycles
Definition Air‑standard cycles are idealized thermodynamic cycles that use air as the working fluid, assuming constant specific heats and reversible processes. They are used to model real engine cycles such as the Otto, Diesel, and Brayton cycles.
Theory [Short] — Key Assumptions of Ideal Air‑Standard Cycles
  1. The working medium is a perfect gas governed by PV=mRT [18].
  2. The mass of the working medium remains strictly constant throughout the cycle [18].
  3. All processes constituting the cycle are internally and externally reversible [18].
  4. Heat is supplied from an external constant high‑temperature source (not chemical combustion) [18].
  5. Heat is rejected to an external constant low‑temperature sink [18].
  6. No heat losses occur between the system and its surroundings [18].
  7. The working medium has constant specific heats (Cp,Cv,γ) throughout [18].
Theory [Explained Simply]

Think of an air‑standard cycle as a "perfect world" version of an engine — no friction, no heat leaks, and air behaves like an ideal gas. It's like a video game with no lag or glitches, so we can calculate the best possible efficiency of an engine.

1.3.2 Thermodynamic Processes Summary

  • Isothermal (T=const): Internal energy ΔU=0; heat added equals work done (Q=W) [19].
  • Adiabatic (Q=0): Governed by PVγ=const; work done causes internal energy change [19].
  • Isentropic (S=const): Reversible adiabatic process with constant entropy [19].
  • Constant Volume / Isochoric (V=const): Boundary work W=0; heat added increases internal energy ΔU=Q=mCvΔT [19, 20].
  • Constant Pressure / Isobaric (P=const): Heat added Q=mCpΔT; work done W=P(V2−V1).

1.3.3 Efficiency Formulae & Derivations

Otto Cycle Thermal Efficiency:

ηOtto = 1 − 1 rγ−1

where r=V1V2 is the compression ratio [3, 4].

Diesel Cycle Thermal Efficiency:

ηDiesel = 1 − 1 rγ−1 [ rcγ−1 γ(rc−1) ]

where rc=V3V2 is the cut‑off ratio [4].

Module 4: Actual Cycles, Engine Losses & Performance Evaluation
Definition Actual engine cycles deviate from ideal air‑standard cycles due to several real‑world factors such as time losses, heat losses, exhaust blowdown, pumping losses, rubbing friction, and specific heat variation. These losses reduce the actual efficiency and power output compared to ideal predictions.
Theory [Short] — Reasons Why Actual Engine Cycles Differ from Ideal Cycles
  1. Time Loss Factor: Combustion requires a definite time for air‑fuel mixing and chemical reaction rather than occurring instantaneously [22].
  2. Heat Loss Factor: Heat is lost to cylinder walls, head, and piston cooling oil [24].
  3. Exhaust Blowdown Loss: Exhaust valve opens 40°–70° before BDC, sacrificing expansion work to reduce backpressure [22, 25].
  4. Pumping Losses: Piston work spent drawing fresh charge past throttling restrictions and pushing out exhaust gases against backpressure [25, 26].
  5. Rubbing Friction Losses: Mechanical friction between piston rings/skirt, bearings, and valvetrain [27].
  6. Specific Heat Variation & Dissociation: Cp and Cv increase with temperature, reducing peak pressure [20].
Theory [Explained Simply]

Imagine a perfect basketball bounce (ideal cycle) versus a real ball that loses energy to friction, heat, and squeaky shoes. Engines lose energy in similar small ways — the exhaust valve opens early, heat escapes through metal, and parts rub together. These small losses add up and reduce the actual work you get out.

Module 5: Marine Fuels, Petroleum Refining & Fuel Ratings
Definition Marine fuels are derived from crude oil through processes like fractional distillation, cracking, reforming, and alkylation. Fuel quality is rated using the Octane Number for SI engines and the Cetane Number for CI engines.
Theory [Short] — Petroleum Refining Processes
  • Fractional Distillation: Separates crude oil into fractions based on boiling point ranges (Fuel gases < 38°C; Gasoline 38–200°C; Diesel/Kerosene 200–350°C; Residuals > 350°C) [28].
  • Thermal & Catalytic Cracking: Breaks heavy, long‑chain hydrocarbon molecules into lighter gasoline/diesel compounds [28].
  • Reformation & Alkylation: Rearranges hydrocarbon structures to increase octane rating [29].
  • Isomerization: Alters structural arrangement without changing chemical formula [29].
Theory [Explained Simply]

Refining crude oil is like sorting a mixed bag of LEGO bricks by size (distillation) and then breaking big bricks into smaller ones (cracking) or snapping them together differently (reforming). Octane and cetane numbers are like "smoothness ratings" — high octane resists knock in petrol engines; high cetane ignites easily in diesel engines.

1.5.2 Fuel Rating Scales

  • Octane Number (SI Engines): Measures resistance to auto‑ignition/knocking. Benchmark fuels: Iso‑octane = 100 (high resistance), n‑heptane = 0 (knocks easily) [29, 30].
  • Cetane Number (CI Engines): Measures ignition quality and short ignition delay. Benchmark fuels: Cetane (n‑hexadecane) = 100 (short delay), 1‑methylnaphthalene = 0 (long delay) [30].
Module 6: Combustion & Knocking in SI vs. CI Engines
Definition SI knock occurs at the end of combustion due to auto‑ignition of the unburnt end‑gas, causing a metallic pinging sound. CI knock occurs at the start of combustion due to a long ignition delay, leading to a sudden pressure rise when accumulated fuel ignites.
Theory [Short] — Four Stages of Combustion in CI Engines
  1. Ignition Delay Period: Physical delay (atomization/vaporization) and chemical delay (pre‑flame reactions) before ignition [39].
  2. Period of Uncontrolled (Rapid) Combustion: Accumulated fuel ignites rapidly, causing a steep pressure rise [40].
  3. Period of Controlled Combustion: Fuel injected burns directly at high pressure/temperature, controlled by injection pump rate [40].
  4. Period of After‑Burning: Unburnt fuel particles continue burning up to 70°–80° after TDC [41].
Theory [Explained Simply]

In a petrol engine, knock is like a loud "ping" at the end of the party when leftover fuel explodes unexpectedly. In a diesel engine, knock is like a rough start — too much fuel accumulates and then fires all at once, causing a harsh bang. Both are bad for the engine and reduce efficiency.

Module 7: Supercharging, Turbocharging & Scavenging
Definition Supercharging is a method of increasing the intake air density by using a mechanically driven compressor (driven from the engine crankshaft). Turbocharging uses an exhaust‑gas‑driven turbine to spin a compressor, recovering waste heat/kinetic energy from exhaust gases without any mechanical connection to the engine crankshaft.
Theory [Short]
  • Supercharging: Boosts intake air density via a mechanical drive from the engine crankshaft (consumes up to 20% engine power) [47, 49]. Types: Centrifugal, Root's, Vane [50, 51].
  • Turbocharger: Driven by an exhaust gas turbine utilizing waste heat/kinetic energy from exhaust gases; no mechanical connection to engine crankshaft [53].
Theory [Explained Simply]

Supercharging is like using a fan powered by the engine itself to blow more air into the cylinder. Turbocharging is like using the engine's own exhaust "wind" to spin a fan — free energy from waste gas!

1.7.2 Scavenging Methods in 2‑Stroke Marine Engines

  • Cross‑Flow Scavenging: Inlet and exhaust ports on opposite sides. Simple and valveless, but leaves unswept corners [54, 55].
  • Loop Scavenging: Inlet and exhaust ports on the same side. Compact, but weakens cylinder liner [56, 57].
  • Uniflow Scavenging: Air enters bottom ports and flows straight up to top exhaust valve. Highest scavenging efficiency and power, but higher cost [57, 58].
Theory [Explained Simply] — Scavenging

Scavenging is simply cleaning out burnt gases. Cross‑flow is like opening two windows on opposite walls; uniflow is like a gentle breeze from bottom to top — most effective.

Module 8: Renewable Ocean Energy, Gas Turbines & Nuclear Power
Definition Marine renewable energy includes wind‑assisted propulsion systems (WAPS), wave energy converters (WEC), and salinity gradient (blue energy). Gas turbines operate on the Brayton cycle, and nuclear power plants use fission heat to produce steam that drives turbines.
Theory [Short] — Renewable Ocean Energy Technologies
  • Wind‑Assisted Propulsion Systems (WAPS): Flettner Rotors (Magnus effect), Rigid Wing Sails, Soft Sails, Kites [74–76].
  • Wave Energy Converters (WEC): Point Absorbers, Attenuators, Oscillating Water Column (OWC), Overtopping Devices [79, 80]. Governing Equation: Power/length≈0.5⋅H2⋅T [kW/m] [77].
  • Salinity Gradient (Blue Energy): Pressure Retarded Osmosis (PRO) and Reverse Electrodialysis (RED) exploiting osmotic pressure across membranes [83, 84].
Theory [Explained Simply]

Wave energy is like capturing the ocean's heartbeat. Gas turbines are jet engines that spin a shaft instead of flying. Nuclear power is a controlled, slow "atomic campfire" that boils water into steam to spin a turbine.

1.8.2 Gas Turbines & Nuclear Power Plants

  • Gas Turbine (Brayton Cycle): Isentropic Compression → Constant Pressure Heat Addition → Isentropic Expansion → Heat Rejection [88, 89].
  • Nuclear Power Plant: Fission of U‑235/Pu‑239 releases heat → Coolant transfers heat to secondary loop → High‑pressure steam drives turbine → Generator produces electricity [93, 94]. Key parts: Reactor core, Fuel rods, Control rods (Boron/Cadmium), Moderator, Containment building [94].

  1. Cylinder Bore: Internal diameter of the engine cylinder.

  1. Suction: Pure air drawn in as piston moves TDC → BDC.
  2. Compression: Air compressed to high pressure/temp (TDC → BDC).
  3. Power: Diesel injected near TDC auto‑ignites, expanding gas drives piston BDC.
  4. Exhaust: Burned gases pushed out as piston moves BDC → TDC.

Question 3 — Valve Timing Diagram, Numerical, Mechanical Losses
3(a) Valve Timing Diagram (5 Marks)

In a four‑stroke engine, the inlet valve opens before TDC (10°–20°) and closes after BDC (40°–60°). The exhaust valve opens before BDC (40°–70°) and closes after TDC (10°–20°). This timing ensures better volumetric efficiency and exhaust scavenging.

Use the indicated power formula:

  • Pumping losses: Drawing fresh charge and pushing out exhaust gases.

Older engines use a carburetor to mix petrol with air in the correct ratio. Modern engines use fuel injection systems (port or direct injection) for precise fuel metering and better efficiency.

Derivation of Diesel Cycle Thermal Efficiency

Heat Added (QS):

QS = m Cp ( T3 − T2 )

Heat Rejected (QR):

QR = m Cv ( T4 − T1 )

Thermal Efficiency:

ηDiesel = 1 − QR QS = 1 − m Cv ( T4 − T1 ) m Cp ( T3 − T2 ) = 1 − 1 γ [ T4 − T1 T3 − T2 ]

Expressing temperatures in terms of compression ratio r=V1/V2 and cut‑off ratio rc=V3/V2:

ηDiesel = 1 − 1 rγ−1 [ rcγ − 1 γ ( rc − 1 ) ]
